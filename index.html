    What is judicial_common_space?¶

    judicial_common_space computes the Judicial Common Space (JCS) score of every appointment of a federal judge who has served since 1953 (the first year of the original study, or any year you choose), following Epstein, Martin, Segal, and Westerland, "The Judicial Common Space," 23 J.L. Econ. & Org. 303 (2007). It saves them in a spreadsheet with each judge's name, the Federal Judicial Center's two numbers for the judge (nid and jid), and the judge's score for each appointment, and checks them against the published scores.

    Why use judicial_common_space?¶

    This package:

    • Scores every Article III appointment: district courts, courts of appeals, and the Supreme Court.
    • Uses the current sources, downloaded when needed: the Federal Judicial Center's Biographical Directory of Article III Federal Judges, Voteview's DW-NOMINATE scores, the congress-legislators dates of senators' service, the Martin-Quinn scores, and Wikipedia's articles on the courts of appeals (for circuit judges' duty stations).
    • Shows its work: each row says which state the judge was appointed from, how that was found, and which senators (or the president) the score came from.
    • Checks itself against the published JCS scores of circuit judges and justices and against Christina Boyd's scores of district judges, which are made the same way.

    How the scores are made¶

    The paper scores the lower courts by the rule of Giles, Hettinger, and Peppers (2001), which follows the norm of senatorial courtesy. If a judge is appointed from a state where the president and at least one senator are of the same party, the judge gets that senator's NOMINATE score, or the mean of the senators' scores. Otherwise the judge gets the president's score. Justices get their Martin-Quinn score in each term, put on the same scale by the paper's arctangent transformation: 2/π · arctan(b0 + b1 · MQ).

    The package applies the rule this way:

    Step How
    Appointment Each row of the center's file of federal judicial service. The senators and the president are those of the nomination (or of the recess appointment, if there was no nomination). A judge renominated by a new president after a recess appointment (as Roger Gregory was) is scored by the president who nominated the judge to the Senate. A judge moved to a court without an appointment (as the Fifth Circuit's judges were to the Eleventh in 1981) keeps the score of the appointment before.
    Home state A district judge's comes from the court's name. A circuit judge's is the state of a district court in the circuit that the judge served on before; or else of the job the judge held when appointed (or the most recent before), as the center's record of the judge's career names it; or else of the judge's duty station, as Wikipedia's article on the court gives it; or else the judge's birth state, if it is in the circuit. The duty station comes after the career because it is where the judge sat, which is sometimes not where the judge came from, but it agrees with the state from the career for 98.5% of the judges who have both. (The state of the judge whom the appointee replaced, from the center's seat numbers, made the matches worse: a seat changes states about one time in five.) Judges of the District of Columbia Circuit, the Federal Circuit, and the other national courts get the president's score, as they do in the published scores.
    Senators By default, every senator from the state who sat in the Congress of the appointment and belonged to the president's party, as the published scores count them (--senators congress). With --senators date, only the senators who held the seats on the day, by their party on that day, which is the rule as the paper states it and closer to Boyd's district scores.
    Scores Voteview's nominate_dim1 for senators and presidents (one score for each member's whole service).
    Justices The arctangent transformation with the coefficients of the 2024 release of the JCS scores (b0 = 0.1934, b1 = 0.6025), recovered from its 782 justice-terms (r = 0.9998), because both scales have changed since the paper estimated b0 = −0.1736 and b1 = 0.461 from the scores of 2005 (--transformation paper uses those). A justice's appointment gets the score of its first term, and the "Justices" sheet has every term.

    How well they match the published scores¶

    With the default settings, against the JCS 2024 release and Boyd's district court scores (through the 118th Congress):

    Check Published Found Same (within 0.0005) Same or within 0.02 Correlation
    Circuit judges (JCS 2024) 705 703 88.3% 96.9% 0.998
    District judges (Boyd) 2,615 2,615 83.6% 95.4% 0.998
    Justice-terms (JCS 2024) 782 782 24.9% 99.4% 1.000

    With --senators date, the district judges match Boyd's scores 99.2% of the time (within 0.02) and the circuit judges match the JCS scores 93.2% of the time, so the two published sources count senators differently.

    Most differences under 0.02 are not errors. Voteview re-estimates the score of every member who is still serving whenever it adds a Congress, so judges whose senators are still in office (Fetterman, Rick Scott, Hickenlooper, and others) drift a little from scores published with an older file. The same is true of recent justices in the Martin-Quinn scores. The larger differences that remain are judges whom the published file places in a state that neither the center's records nor Wikipedia show (such as Richard Cudahy in Illinois and Stephen Trott in Idaho), a few D.C. Circuit judges and the Federal Circuit judges who came from the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals, which the published file scores differently, and some possible slips in the published files. The spreadsheet's check sheets list every comparison, and the next section explains every one that differs by more than 0.05.

    Using the published scores¶

    --use-published (off by default) gives a judge or a justice's term the published score wherever it differs from the computed one by more than 0.05 (or --published-threshold): Epstein et al.'s for circuit judges and justices, and Boyd's for district judges. The Scores and Justices sheets then say where each score came from (jcs_source) and keep the computed score beside it (computed_jcs); the check sheets still compare the computed scores. With the default settings, it replaces the scores of 95 appointments since 1953 (80 district judges, 14 circuit judges, and Justice Jackson's appointment, from her first term) and of two justice-terms; with --senators date, it replaces 46 (13 district judges, 32 circuit judges, and Justice Jackson's).

    Use it to reproduce the published scores, as when a study must line up with earlier work that used them. It is off by default because:

    • The two sources count senators differently (Boyd by the day, Epstein et al. by the Congress), so taking both mixes their rules: with either setting of --senators, one of them is overruled.
    • Some of the published scores that it would take look like slips, such as John Nalbandian's, Morris Arnold's, James Connell's, and the six New York and Kentucky judges'.
    • Only judges in the published files can be replaced, so judges appointed in the same circumstances can end up with scores made by different rules.
    • The published scores were made with older Voteview files, so they lag behind the current scores of senators who are still serving.

    Getting started¶

    Requirements¶

    • Python 3.11 or later
    • An internet connection, the first time, to download the source files (about 25 megabytes)

    Installation¶

    To install judicial_common_space, use pip:

    pip install judicial-common-space

    Usage¶

    From the command line:

    judicial-common-space --output judicial_common_space.xlsx

    Or from Python:

    import judicial_common_space

judicial_common_space.run('judicial_common_space.xlsx', start_year = 1953)
    Option Default Description
    --output judicial_common_space.xlsx Where to save the spreadsheet.
    --start-year 1953 Include judges who served on a court in this year or later, and the justices' terms from the one sitting in January of that year. The default is the first year of the original study, which covered 1953 through 2000; the published scores now run through 2024, and these through the latest data.
    --senators congress Which senators count: congress (as the published scores do) or date (those holding the seats on the day).
    --transformation jcs2024 How Martin-Quinn scores are put on the scale: jcs2024 or paper.
    --martin-quinn the data folder's The Martin-Quinn justices.csv, if it cannot be downloaded. The Martin-Quinn site checks for automated browsers, so if the download fails, save the file from its page and pass its path (or put it in the data folder's politics folder).
    --data-folder data/courts beside the code folder Where the downloads are kept: the same folders that courtpy uses, so the projects share them. Set JUDICIAL_COMMON_SPACE_DATA_DIR to change it for good.
    --overwrite off Download the files again, as when the center has added judges or Voteview has added votes.
    --no-download off Use only the files that are already there.
    --use-published off Use the published score (Epstein et al.'s for circuit judges and justices, Boyd's for district judges) where it differs from the computed one by more than the threshold (see Using the published scores).
    --published-threshold 0.05 The largest difference from a published score that keeps the computed score, with --use-published.
    --no-check off Do not compare the scores with the published scores.

    The spreadsheet has these sheets:

    Sheet Contents
    Scores A row for each appointment: judge, nid, jid, the court, the president and party, the dates, the Congress, home_state (a postal code) and state_source (how it was found), duty_station (Wikipedia's state for a circuit judge), method and senators (how the score was found), president_score, computed_jcs, jcs, and jcs_source ("computed", or with --use-published, "Epstein et al." or "Boyd").
    Justices Each justice's Martin-Quinn and JCS scores in each term, with computed_jcs and jcs_source.
    Verification The agreement with each set of published scores.
    Circuit check, District check, Justice check Every comparison, with both scores and how the computed one was found.
    About The method, the sources, and the settings.
